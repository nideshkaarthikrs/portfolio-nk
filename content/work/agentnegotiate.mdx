## The problem

Small teams that buy the same categories of goods repeatedly — raw materials, parts, packaging —
spend a disproportionate amount of time haggling over purchases that follow the same pattern
every time. Someone has to chase quotes, push back on price and stay inside a budget, and very
little of it gets recorded anywhere useful.

The question behind AgentNegotiate: can two AI agents run that back-and-forth on their own,
while the money decisions stay under rules a human actually trusts?

## What I built

AgentNegotiate is a simulation prototype, not a live product. Both sides of the deal are agents
configured in the app, and payments run in test mode. It exists to show how the negotiation
loop and its guardrails behave end to end.

You set a mandate for each side: the buyer's target price, maximum budget and round limit, and
the seller's list price, floor and round limit. A plain-English procurement request ("100 units
of steel bolts, 30-day payment terms") is parsed into structured data by the LLM.

The agents then trade offers round by round, each with a short explanation of its move. When
they converge, the deal closes and a payment link can be generated for the agreed amount.

Every offer, decision and payment event is written to an audit trail where each entry carries
the hash of the one before it, so any tampering breaks the chain.

## Architecture

<ArchitectureDiagram slug="agentnegotiate" />

**Key decisions and trade-offs**

- **The LLM never makes financial decisions.** Accept, counter and every price come from a
  deterministic policy engine. The LLM only parses the procurement request and writes the
  human-readable explanations. That keeps the money logic testable, at the cost of the agents
  sounding a little formulaic.
- **Hard limits are applied last.** The buyer's counter can never push the total past the
  budget, and the seller's never drops below the floor — no other rule can override them.
- **A shared round horizon.** The negotiation ends at whichever side's round limit is lower, so
  one side can't keep conceding past a limit the other never agreed to. Each round concedes a
  growing share of the remaining gap, and the final round concedes all of it.
- **Hash-chained audit, not just logs.** Chaining entries with SHA-256 makes the trail
  tamper-evident, so a human can trust how a deal was reached, not just that it was.

## Where it's going

For now this stays a prototype. Before turning it into anything more, I want to understand how
procurement teams actually negotiate today and where they'd let an agent act on their behalf.
